My rules

  • Hit 140+ g of protein in two or three meals.
  • No alcohol, no fried food, no sugary drinks.
  • Vegetables or fruit at every meal.
  • Water all day, electrolytes on long or hot days.
  • Walk 10 minutes after every meal.
  • Meal-prep on Sunday so there's never a reason to grab junk.

The Four Burners

The old theory says turn one off to succeed. I say turn the flame down, never off, and check every burner each Sunday.

Family

One undistracted meal a day. A walk with the kids every evening. Date night every other week.

Minimum: one real meal together

Friends

Sunday walk-and-talk with friends. One call a week to an old battle buddy.

Minimum: one real conversation a week

Health

5 miles a day, strength training 3 times a week, 16/8 eating, 7+ hours of sleep.

Minimum: 3 miles on the worst day

Work

Lead well, do the hard work early, coach the team, leave work at work.

Minimum: protect focus, end on time

The Sunday Burner Check

  1. Rate each burner from 1 to 5.
  2. Pick the lowest one.
  3. Choose one small action for it this week.
  4. Stack burners when you can: a walk with your kid is Family and Health at once.

Where the Four Burners comes from

The original

A story told on an Australian road trip

The idea was popularized by writer David Sedaris in his 2009 New Yorker essay "Laugh, Kookaburra." On a road trip through Australia, a friend who had retired after a successful career described life as a stove with four burners: family, friends, health and work.

The lesson was blunt. To be successful, you have to turn one burner off. To be very successful, you have to turn off two.

How it's grown

From "turn one off" to "seasons"

James Clear, author of Atomic Habits, helped spread the theory and suggested ways to live with it: hand off or automate what you can, set clear limits on your ambition, and turn burners up or down through different seasons of life.

Greg McKeown's book Essentialism makes a related case. Trade-offs are unavoidable, so choose them on purpose and focus your energy on what matters most.

My take

Turn it down. Never turn it off.

I agree that you can't run all four burners on high at once. But I've seen what happens when one goes out completely. When I let my health go cold for years, it nearly took the other three with it. So I keep every burner lit, give each one a minimum flame I never go below, and turn them up or down as the season demands. That's the whole idea behind Keep All Four Lit.

The Science

Every habit I teach is backed by research. Here's what it says, without the hype.

Fasting, simply explained

Fasting just means going a set time without food. People do it for health, discipline or faith. Here are three common kinds.

Intermittent fasting

Eat in a window

You cycle between eating and not eating. Popular versions are 16:8 (eat within 8 hours), 5:2 (two low-calorie days a week) and alternate-day fasting.

Studies show it can help with weight and blood sugar, mostly because a shorter window makes it easier to eat less. It works about as well as plain calorie cutting, so pick whichever you can stick with.

de Cabo & Mattson, NEJM, 2019 · Liu et al., NEJM, 2022
Ramadan fasting

Dawn to sunset, for a month

During Ramadan, Muslims fast from dawn to sunset for about 30 days, with no food or water, as an act of faith. Meals are eaten before dawn (suhoor) and after sunset (iftar).

Research shows modest weight loss during the month that often returns afterward. The big health tip: drink plenty of water and eat slow-digesting food at suhoor to get through the day.

Fernando et al., Nutrients, 2019
36-hour fasting

A full day and a half

You stop eating after dinner and don't eat again until breakfast two days later, drinking only water, black coffee or plain tea.

By this point your body has used most of its stored sugar and runs mainly on fat. It's an advanced step: keep up salt and fluids, break it gently, and talk to your doctor first.

Cahill, Annual Review of Nutrition, 2006

What happens after you eat

Food is broken down into fuel. Here's the simple version of what your body does, hour by hour, from your last bite.

0–4 hrs

Digesting

Carbs become glucose (sugar), protein becomes amino acids, fat becomes fatty acids. Blood sugar rises, and insulin moves that sugar into your cells.

4–12 hrs

Using stored sugar

Insulin falls. Your liver releases glycogen, its stored sugar, to keep your energy steady.

12–24 hrs

Switching to fat

Glycogen runs low, so your body burns more fat. The liver starts turning fat into ketones, a backup fuel.

24–36+ hrs

Running on fat

Ketones climb and become a main fuel, including for your brain. This is the deep end of fasting.

Times are rough guides. They shift with what you ate, how active you are and your body.

What to eat first, especially in the morning

Whether it's breakfast or the meal that breaks your fast, what you eat first sets up the rest of your day.

Spike and crash

Sugary or refined carbs

Pastries, sugary cereal, juice, white bread. They turn into sugar fast, blood sugar shoots up, insulin floods in, then you crash and feel hungry and tired again within a couple of hours.

Steady energy

Protein, fiber and healthy fats

Eggs, Greek yogurt, oats, berries, nuts, avocado. They digest slowly, so blood sugar rises gently and you stay full and focused for hours.

The research behind my habits

Steps

More steps, longer life

Risk of early death drops as daily steps rise, leveling off around 8,000–10,000.

Paluch et al., Lancet Public Health, 2022
After meals

Walk after you eat

Short walks after meals lower blood-sugar spikes.

Buffey et al., Sports Medicine, 2022
Strength

Muscle is medicine

Strength training keeps muscle as you age and lowers mortality.

Momma et al., Br J Sports Med, 2022
Protein

About 1.6 g per kg

That much daily protein supports muscle built through training.

Morton et al., Br J Sports Med, 2018
Sleep

7 hours minimum

Adults need at least 7 hours of sleep a night.

AASM and SRS consensus, 2015
Whole foods

Processed food makes you eat more

People on ultra-processed diets ate about 500 more calories a day.

Hall et al., Cell Metabolism, 2019
Friendship

Friends add years

Strong social ties are linked to a longer life.

Holt-Lunstad et al., PLOS Medicine, 2010
About me

Hi, I'm Coach E.

I grew up on three continents, and in the inner cities of most of the countries I lived in, I learned fútbol on the streets and local pitches. The game taught me teamwork, grit, and how to connect with people anywhere.

At 23, I joined the Army. More than 20 years later, I'm still on active duty, and that time has shaped me as much as the game did. Leading thousands of soldiers taught me how to talk to anyone, earn trust, and bring out the best in people. I learned that the best leaders are mentors and coaches first, and that a team only wins when everyone on it does.

Along the way, I earned a master's in Applied Physiology and Kinesiology from the University of Florida. I don't just live the science of performance and health. I've studied it.

But by my early 40s, I'd let it slide. I was heavier, prediabetic, and having a drink every night to unwind. I didn't want my kids to remember that version of me. So I started walking, quit drinking, and rebuilt the way I eat.

Today, I'm in the best shape of my life. I coach youth soccer and raise three kids with my wife. And I share what works so you can do it too.

Let's get to work.
— Coach E
